= Module 3: Full Automation — AI-Assisted Remediation
:navtitle: Module 3: Full Automation

[WARNING]
====
This module is a *work in progress*. Steps and screenshots may change before the final version.
====

*Duration:* 30 minutes

Module 2 ended with a deliberate limitation: Renovate found one dependency in one repository and asked you to merge it. That works until the day Lightwell publishes forty remediations across sixty repositories, and "ask a human to merge it" stops being a strategy.

This module walks through the same remediation event, handled by the platform instead of by you. An Automation Orchestrator workflow opens a ServiceNow incident, asks Trusted Profile Analyzer which applications are actually affected, applies each application's own approval policy, and dispatches an AI agent to write the merge request. The pipeline then tests the change and writes the results back into the merge request body, so that when a human *is* asked to decide, they are reading evidence rather than guessing.

You will remediate two of your three applications:

* `benefits-mgmt-app` — internal HR application, risk class `LOW`, policy `auto_approve`. The platform merges it without you.
* `wire-transfer-svc` — payment-critical and PCI-scoped, risk class `HIGH`, policy `needs_approval`. The platform does everything *except* the last step, and then waits for you.

That contrast is the point of the module. Automation is not about removing the human — it is about making sure the human is only interrupted when their judgement is actually worth something.

== Foundations

Before diving into the hands-on steps, this section compares what changes between the quick win approach and the full automation approach, introduces the orchestration pipeline and the two applications you will remediate — one low-risk that merges automatically, one higher-risk that waits for your approval.

[cols="1,2,2",options="header"]
|===
| Stage | Quick Win (Module 2) | Full Automation (this module)

| Trigger
| Renovate polls the artifact manager on a schedule
| The artifact manager fires a `component CREATED` webhook the moment the artifact lands

| Who finds the affected apps
| Renovate reads one repository's `pom.xml`
| TPA is queried for every SBOM containing the component

| Who writes the fix
| Renovate's regex manager
| The `impact-analyzer` AI agent (OpenCode)

| What the merge request says
| A one-line version bump
| CVE, CVSS, risk class, and a validation table filled in by the pipeline

| Who approves
| Always you
| Per-app policy — `auto_approve` or `needs_approval`

| Audit trail
| Git history only
| A ServiceNow incident, commented at every stage

| Build, sign, deploy
| Shared — Tekton, OCI registry, ArgoCD, TPA
| Shared — identical
|===

The back half of both paths is the same. Full automation only replaces *who decides what to change and whether it may ship*.

== The Orchestrated Pipeline

Event-Driven Ansible is deliberately thin here. It is a sensor: it recognises the `.rhlw` artifact event and triggers a job template, which posts to an Automation Orchestrator webhook. Everything after that is a node on an Orchestrator canvas, recorded as it runs.

Your project contains *three* workflows, not one, and the split is the first thing worth understanding:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Workflow | Runs when

| *Lightwell Remediation — Start*
| A remediated artifact is published. Ends when the merge requests are ready.

| *— Merged*
| A merge request is merged — by you or by the Orchestrator. Follows the build through.

| *— Deploy*
| ArgoCD reports the application healthy. Closes the incident out.
|===

They are separate because the Orchestrator cannot pause a run and resume it on an outside event. Rather than hold a job open for the hours a human review might take, each stage is its own short execution, woken by a webhook. The ServiceNow incident is what ties them back into one story.

It is one Ansible job running several plays, so the canvas shows you the decision points rather than the plumbing.

The merge — by the Orchestrator or by you — is where the full automation path rejoins the quick win path:

The *Merged* workflow does not get a webhook when the build finishes — Tekton does not send one. Instead it polls the GitLab commit status that Pipelines-as-Code already mirrors from the PipelineRun. That is why one Merged execution can run for several minutes: it is watching your build.

== The Incident Opens Before the Change Does

Look at the order again. Opening the parent incident is the *first* thing *Prepare Remediation* does — before TPA is queried, before anything is classified, before a single line of anyone's `pom.xml` is read. The platform raises the ticket and then does the work, which is the opposite of how most automation behaves.

Switch to the https://snow-mock-snow-{guid}.{openshift_cluster_ingress_domain}[ServiceNow^] (no login required). Your incident is at the top of the list, with the most recent state.

What you are looking at is a small hierarchy, not one ticket:

* A *parent* incident for the remediation event itself — one artifact, one Lightwell publication, one thread to follow.
* A *child* incident per affected application, created straight after classify, once the Orchestrator knows which applications are genuinely in scope.

The children are where the detail goes. Each application's loop iteration comments on its own child as it completes — merge request opened, verifier passed, awaiting merge — so two applications remediated from one event do not interleave their history into a single unreadable timeline. The parent gets short milestone notes with links down into the children, and nothing else. An operator reads the parent to find out *where things are*, and opens a child to find out *what happened*.

The comments are not just prose, either. Open one and the merge request, the pipeline run and (later) the ArgoCD application and the running route are all clickable, so the ticket is a working console rather than a transcript.

Your two incidents are at the top of the list, both raised by *Lightwell AO*. Open the parent — the one whose description starts with *remediate*:

image::m3-snow-parent-incident.png["The parent incident, showing the blast-radius classification and the child tickets it opened",link=_images/m3-snow-parent-incident.png,window=_images]

== The Merge Request the Agent Wrote

In the loop, the Orchestrator tasks the `impact-analyzer` agent with creating the fix. The agent reads the application's `pom.xml`, bumps the dependency to the remediated `.rhlw` version, pushes a branch, and opens a merge request.

Open https://gitlab-gitlab.{openshift_cluster_ingress_domain}[GitLab^] and go to `acme-{guid}/benefits-mgmt-app` → *Merge requests*. The title follows a fixed shape:

[source,text]
----
[LW] 🔴 Bump h2 to 2.2.224.rhlw-70003 to fix CVE-2023-48795
----

That example is from a real `wire-transfer-svc` run — yours will name the dependency you just published. The coloured dot encodes severity, so a reviewer scanning a list of twenty merge requests can triage before opening any of them.

The diff itself is almost boring — the same single-line `pom.xml` change Renovate made in Module 2. The interesting part is the description, which is the artifact a human is actually expected to read:

[cols="1,3",options="header"]
|===
| Section | What it carries

| Summary
| Package, current version, remediated version, a link to the CVE, CVSS score, Lightwell risk score, and the SBOM impact

| Vulnerability Details
| What the CVE is and how the remediated build addresses it

| Automated Validation
| An eight-row table of checks. At this point every row reads `Pending` — nothing has been tested yet

| Risk Assessment
| The application's Acme Inc. risk class and the rationale from its policy file

| Recommended Action
| Derived, not guessed: a failed check means do not merge; a `HIGH` or `CRITICAL` risk class means human review even when everything passes
|===

== The Pipeline Fills in the Validation Table

The merge request is created before any testing has happened, so every row in the *Automated Validation* table starts as `Pending`. A Pipelines-as-Code pipeline, `build-pull-request`, runs against the open merge request, executes `mvn verify` together with the CycloneDX SBOM plugin, and then rewrites that one section of the description with real results.

Refresh the merge request after a minute or two and the table will have filled in:

[cols="2,3",options="header"]
|===
| Check | What it actually measures

| Acme Inc. Risk Class
| The `riskClass` from the application's policy file

| Binary signature verification
| An *integrity* check — the published JAR is compared against the checksum the artifact manager recorded for it. The lab mirror ships no detached GPG signature, so this is not a cryptographic signature check and is labelled accordingly.

| SBOM integrity
| A CycloneDX SBOM is generated in the same build and asserted to list the bumped coordinate

| Unit tests
| `surefire` results from `mvn verify`

| Integration tests
| `failsafe` results from the same run

| Dependency compatibility
| Asserts that what the `pom.xml` declares is what the build actually resolved

| Regression scan
| *Not implemented.* This row stays `Pending`.

| License compliance
| Compares the licence declared by the base POM against the remediated POM, normalised so that equivalent phrasings do not raise a false alarm
|===

image::m3-mr-validation-filled.png["The same table after build-pull-request has run: six checks Passed, Regression scan still Pending",link=_images/m3-mr-validation-filled.png,window=_images]

IMPORTANT: Six of those eight rows are measured. *Regression scan is not implemented* and renders as `Pending` — a behavioural diff would mean running the whole suite against the target-branch baseline as well, roughly doubling build time, and that call has not been made yet. Nothing here is ever reported green without having been measured, which is the only reason the table is worth putting in front of a reviewer at all.

== The Low-Risk Application Merges Itself

Back on the Orchestrator canvas, the *MR Verifier* node confirms the merge request is healthy and every check reported, then the *Auto-approve?* condition reads the approval value that travelled with the application's own policy.

For `benefits-mgmt-app` that value is `auto_approve`, so the canvas takes the green branch to *Merge MR (auto-approved)*. The Orchestrator marks the merge request ready and merges it. No human is asked, and no human is notified. The child incident gets a comment recording the merge, and the loop moves on to the next application.

Go back to GitLab and open the merge request again. It is already merged, and the merge was performed by the automation account — you never touched it:

image::m3-mr-auto-merged.png["The merge request now showing Merged, with the full Summary table intact",link=_images/m3-mr-auto-merged.png,window=_images]

Check the timestamps while you are there. The merge happened roughly two minutes after the request was opened, not seconds — the Orchestrator was waiting for `build-pull-request` to report before it merged.

This is the volume case, and it is the whole economic argument for the platform. An internal HR application, a compatible rebuild of a library, every automated check green: there is no decision here that a person adds value to, so no person is asked. Multiply that by forty remediations a week and the time saved is not the merge click — it is the context switch that surrounds it.

[NOTE]
====
The decision is made from `.lightwell/policy.json` in the application's own repository, and it fails closed. If that file is missing, unreadable or malformed, the Orchestrator treats the application as `needs_approval` and routes it to a human. Nothing merges itself by accident.
====

== The Judgement Call

On the canvas, the *Auto-approve?* condition does not see `auto_approve`, so it takes the other branch: a comment on the child incident recording that the merge request is ready and *awaiting a GitLab merge*. Then the execution ends.

Read that again, because it is easy to misread as a failure. The Orchestrator has not crashed, timed out, or parked a job that is burning a worker somewhere. It has done everything it is permitted to do and stopped. The merge request is open in GitLab with your name on it, and nothing further happens in this entire platform until a person acts.

[IMPORTANT]
====
*The pause lives in GitLab, not in the Orchestrator.* There is no approve button on the canvas and no task in a queue waiting for you to tick it. The human gate is the merge request itself — the artifact that actually carries the evidence — rather than a prompt in an automation tool that quotes a ticket number at you.
====

Check ServiceNow first. The child incident for `wire-transfer-svc` records that the merge request is ready and hands the decision to you — *please review and merge in GitLab when you are satisfied; CI/CD will continue from there*:

== The Incident Closes Last

Go back to the https://snow-mock-snow-{guid}.{openshift_cluster_ingress_domain}[ServiceNow^] tab you left open.

The parent incident is now *Resolved*. Read its thread from the top — it is deliberately short, a sequence of milestones with links down into the children:

. The incident was raised the moment the artifact manager announced the artifact — before anything was known about who was affected
. Child tickets were opened, one per application TPA said was genuinely affected
. Each application reached a reviewable merge request
. Merge, build and deploy were each noted as they landed
. The parent moved to *Resolved* once ArgoCD reported the application Healthy

Now open a child. That is where the work actually is: the merge request link, the CVE, the verifier outcome, the pipeline run, the ArgoCD application and the route the fixed code is now serving on. The parent tells an operator where things stand; the child tells them what happened.

Notice what resolved it. Not the merge, and not a green pipeline — the *deploy*. `SNOW finalize` only runs in the Deploy workflow, so the ticket stays open until the remediated code is actually running. A build that passes and never ships does not close anything.

That is also why three workflows rather than one long-running job. The Start run ends when the merge requests are ready; merge and deploy arrive later as webhooks and start their own short executions. The incident is what stitches them back into a single story — which is precisely the job a ticket is supposed to do.

NOTE: The child incidents stay open. Only the parent is resolved today; per-application close on deploy success is not wired up. If you are demonstrating this to an auditor, show them the parent and let them click down.

[TIP]
====
This is the slide that usually lands in front of a risk or compliance team. Nobody had to be asked for the evidence, and nobody had to assemble it afterwards. The incident was open before the change existed, it accumulated every decision and measurement along the way, and it closed when the fix was live — produced entirely as a side effect of doing the work.
====

== Summary

You have just run the *Full Automation* path end to end:

. You published a remediated artifact; the artifact manager raised a `component CREATED` event
. Event-Driven Ansible acted as a sensor and started the Orchestrator workflow
. The Orchestrator opened a ServiceNow incident before proposing any change
. TPA answered which applications were genuinely affected, from SBOM data
. Each application was classified against its own `.lightwell/policy.json`
. The `impact-analyzer` agent opened a merge request carrying CVE data, risk class and a validation table
. The `build-pull-request` pipeline tested the change and wrote the real results back into that table
. `benefits-mgmt-app` was auto-approved and merged by the Orchestrator, without you
. `wire-transfer-svc` passed every check, and its merge request still said human review was required, so it waited for you to merge it in GitLab
. Both were built, signed, promoted and deployed by the same pipeline and ArgoCD
. The parent incident resolved once ArgoCD reported the deployment Healthy, carrying a complete, timestamped trail

=== What This Actually Buys You

The difference from the quick win path is not "AI wrote the merge request". It is three things:

* *Scope* — the system knows which applications contain the component, because it asked TPA rather than scanning a single repository.
* *Evidence* — by the time a human looks at the change, the tests have run and the results are in the merge request. The reviewer reads measurements, not promises.
* *Proportionality* — attention is spent where it changes the outcome. An internal HR application merges itself. A PCI-scoped payment service does not, and no amount of green ticks changes that.

Scale is what makes this necessary. One remediation a week is a Renovate problem. Forty a week across sixty repositories, each needing a risk judgement and an audit trail, is an orchestration problem — and the only way a human stays in the loop at that volume is if the loop stops bothering them about `benefits-mgmt-app`.
